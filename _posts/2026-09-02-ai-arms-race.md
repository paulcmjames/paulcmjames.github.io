> I ask Chatgpt to analyse <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/sep/01/from-bill-gates-to-bernie-sanders-most-agree-the-ai-arms-race-is-disastrous-only-europe-can-make-it-stop" target="_blank">Guardian article on the AI arms race</a> which says there is a lack of incentive to slow down AI development but a potential race breaker in AMSL - an advanced chip maker in Europe. AI suggests the solutions is more nuanced and we should focus more on who controls the money, security and power when AI takes over.

### The real question is not whether AI should advance, but who controls its direction, speed and distribution of benefits.

The article is right that AI development is increasingly governed by competitive dynamics that make voluntary restraint extremely unlikely. But I think it overstates both Europe's ability to “stop” the race and the usefulness of simply pausing AI development. The real question is not whether AI should advance, but who controls its direction, speed and distribution of benefits.

The article's argument is essentially that the AI race is producing a strange consensus: people across the political spectrum increasingly agree that uncontrolled AI development could be socially disastrous, yet nobody seems capable of slowing it down. Its proposed answer is that Europe has a unique piece of leverage through ASML and its advanced chipmaking technology.

### 1. I think the article is absolutely right about the race dynamic

This is the most important part.

Nobody really has an incentive to slow down.

+ OpenAI worries about Google and Anthropic.
+ Google worries about OpenAI and China.
+ China worries about the US.
+ Governments worry about losing economic and military advantage.
+ Investors worry about missing the next trillion-dollar company.

So even if everyone privately agrees that slowing down would be sensible, individually they have strong incentives to continue.

That's a classic collective-action problem.

It's rather like climate change, except potentially faster: everyone benefits from restraint, but each individual actor fears being the one who restrains themselves while everyone else carries on.

And I think this is particularly relevant to something we've discussed before: AI isn't simply another technology being introduced into society gradually. The speed matters enormously.

Society normally gets decades to adapt to technological change. With AI, potentially huge changes to:

+ professional employment
+ education
+ intellectual property
+ government
+ warfare
+ surveillance
+ business formation

could happen within a relatively short period.

The Guardian article is right to say that our institutions are adapting much more slowly than the technology.

### 2. But I think the article bundles together several very different AI risks

This is where I'm slightly less convinced.

It puts things like:

+ mass unemployment
+ environmental damage
+ authoritarian surveillance
+ autonomous weapons
+ loss of control over superintelligent AI

into one big category: “AI is dangerous, therefore stop the AI race.”

But these problems require quite different solutions.

For example:

Mass unemployment - a pause might delay the problem, but doesn't solve it.

### The deeper question is: Who owns the AI that replaces human labour?

If AI dramatically increases productivity, society could become enormously richer. But if the ownership of that productive capacity remains concentrated in a handful of corporations and investors, you get:

AI productivity boom → wealth concentration → unemployment/insecurity → political instability.

That's the real danger.

And this is where I think the article doesn't go far enough.

The future problem may not be: “There isn't enough wealth.” It may be: “There is enormous wealth, but increasingly fewer people have access to the mechanisms that generate it.”

That is a fundamentally political and economic problem.

### 3. The ASML argument is clever — but probably less powerful than the article suggests

The most interesting specific argument is that Europe has a choke point because of ASML's extreme ultraviolet lithography machines.

The argument is basically: Europe controls a critical bottleneck in advanced chip production, therefore Europe could force a global slowdown.

There is definitely something real here. ASML has extraordinary strategic importance in the semiconductor supply chain, and chipmaking equipment has already become part of geopolitical competition.

But I think there are three problems.

### First: Europe doesn't really operate ASML in a geopolitical vacuum

The US has enormous influence over the semiconductor ecosystem.

A European attempt to use ASML to impose a broad global AI pause would create massive political conflict with the United States, Japan, Taiwan and South Korea, and potentially other partners.

### Second: you can slow frontier AI without stopping AI

The technology already exists.

Even if you dramatically restricted the most advanced chips, there would still be:

+ existing data centres
+ existing GPUs
+ older-generation chips
+ smaller models
+ open-weight models
+ algorithmic improvements

So this isn't really like putting a genie back in the bottle.

### Third: chokepoints don't necessarily last

The article itself recognises that Europe's technological advantage may not last forever. If Europe tried to weaponise that monopoly aggressively, other countries would have an enormous incentive to develop alternatives. So ASML gives Europe leverage, but probably not permanent control.

### 4. Where I think the article is most compelling is its implicit argument about democracy

This, for me, is the biggest issue - we are currently allowing a tiny number of companies to make decisions that could reshape the following at extraordinary speed:

+ employment
+ education
+ media
+ knowledge
+ government
+ warfare

And these companies are primarily accountable to:

+ shareholders
+ investors
+ competitive pressure

not democratic publics.

That's quite remarkable when you think about it.

Imagine if in the 19th century a handful of private companies had been allowed to decide how quickly should we industrialise society, or in the 20th century, how quickly should nuclear technology proliferate? There would have been an assumption that governments had some role in managing the transition.

With AI, we've somehow accepted the idea that the companies building the technology should largely determine how fast society adopts it.

That's the bit I find genuinely disturbing.

### 5. I actually think there are two AI debates happening simultaneously

And people constantly confuse them.

### Debate 1: “Will AI become uncontrollable?”

This is the AGI/superintelligence/alignment debate.

Questions like:

+ Can an AI deceive us?
+ Can it evade human control?
+ Could it develop goals incompatible with ours?

That's the kind of issue covered in the other Guardian piece today about increasingly capable AI systems exhibiting deceptive behaviour.

This is a genuine technical safety problem.

### Debate 2: “What happens if AI works extremely well?”

I actually think this is the more immediate problem.

Suppose there is no Terminator scenario.

Suppose AI behaves perfectly.

Suppose it simply becomes extraordinarily good at:

+ coding
+ law
+ design
+ accounting
+ medicine
+ administration
+ customer service
+ management

Then what? That's potentially revolutionary. And I think this is where someone like you is interesting, because you're sitting in exactly the kind of territory where AI could be enormously transformative.

Your work in service design isn't simply a manual task that disappears overnight. But a huge amount of the surrounding cognitive labour can potentially be automated:

+ research synthesis
+ interview analysis
+ mapping
+ drafting
+ requirements analysis
+ documentation
+ generating options
+ prototyping

So the likely first effect isn't “Paul is replaced by AI.” It's: “One very capable service designer using AI can potentially do the work that previously required several people.”

And that's economically just as disruptive.

The danger isn't necessarily zero jobs. It's that the relationship between productivity and employment breaks down.

### 6. My biggest disagreement with the article: I don't think “make it stop” is the right objective

I understand the emotional appeal of it. But I don't think we can realistically stop this. And more importantly, I'm not sure we'd want to.

AI could potentially help enormously with:

+ scientific discovery
+ medicine
+ climate modelling
+ government services
+ education
+ accessibility
+ small businesses
+ productivity

The question should be: Can we slow down particular capabilities and deployments while society catches up? That's a much more realistic objective.

For example, I would distinguish between:

🟢 Encourage
+ medical research
+ scientific AI
+ accessibility tools
+ productivity tools
+ government service improvement
+ education assistance

🟠 Regulate heavily
+ employment automation at scale
+ AI decision-making about citizens
+ surveillance
+ political persuasion
+ financial systems
+ critical infrastructure

🔴 Potentially prohibit or internationally control
+ autonomous lethal weapons
+ certain biological/chemical design capabilities
+ AI systems operating critical infrastructure without meaningful oversight
+ highly autonomous systems capable of cyberattack or self-propagation

That's a much more sophisticated approach than simply: AI = good or AI = bad.

My overall view is I think the Guardian article is directionally right but strategically too simplistic. Its strongest argument is that we are allowing competitive and commercial incentives to determine the speed of a civilisation-scale technological transition. I think that's absolutely correct.

Its weakest argument is that Europe can simply pull the ASML lever and make the world stop. Probably not.

The solution I would favour is something closer to:

+ Slow the frontier where risks are highest.
+ Regulate deployment rather than trying to regulate every form of AI equally.
+ Make companies bear some responsibility for the social costs of automation.
+ Ensure that the productivity gains from AI are distributed beyond the owners of the AI infrastructure.

Because I increasingly think that is the central political question of the next 20 years. Not “Will AI make us richer?” I think it probably will.
But who gets the money when human labour becomes less economically necessary?

And I suspect that's the question the world is spectacularly unprepared for.

The timing of this debate is particularly interesting because the US is reportedly pushing a much more hands-off approach to AI regulation internationally, which illustrates exactly the competitive political dynamic the Guardian piece is worried about.
